What is the effectiveness of PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy compared to combination therapy as first-line treatment for PD-L1-high metastatic non-small
cell lung cancer in terms of overall survival and progression-free survival?
In PD-L1-high metastatic NSCLC, PD-1 inhibitor–chemotherapy combination therapy consistently improves
progression-free survival over PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy, but overall survival is similar between the two
strategies, with combination therapy carrying greater toxicity — though this conclusion rests entirely on indirect
comparisons, as no direct head-to-head randomized trial has been completed.
Abstract
Across 18 included sources — predominantly network meta-analyses and indirect comparisons of randomized controlled trials — PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy and PD-1 inhibitor–chemotherapy combination therapy both significantly improve overall survival and progression-free survival relative to chemotherapy alone in patients with PDL1-high (TPS ≥50%) metastatic NSCLC [1–3]. Combination therapy consistently demonstrates superior progressionfree survival compared with monotherapy, with hazard ratios ranging from 0.55 to 0.81 across analyses [2, 4–7].
However, no statistically significant overall survival difference between the two strategies has been identified in the
PD-L1-high subgroup: median OS is approximately 22–26 months with either approach [2, 4, 8, 9]. Combination
therapy is associated with higher rates of grade 3–5 treatment-related adverse events [9–11].
No completed randomized trial has directly compared PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy with PD-1 inhibitor–
chemotherapy in PD-L1-high disease; all comparisons are indirect, anchored through a chemotherapy common
comparator [2, 4, 5]. The two available head-to-head RCTs evaluated other combination partners (ipilimumab
and epacadostat) added to pembrolizumab and found no efficacy benefit [10, 12]. Consequently, whether the PFS
advantage of chemo-immunotherapy translates into a meaningful long-term survival benefit in PD-L1-high patients,
or whether monotherapy provides equivalent OS with lower toxicity, remains formally unresolved. Critical gaps
include the absence of validated predictive biomarkers beyond PD-L1 TPS for differential treatment selection [9,
13], insufficient safety and quality-of-life data stratified by PD-L1 expression [6, 8], and underrepresentation of key
clinical subgroups such as patients with liver metastases, brain metastases, or advanced age [1, 14].

Summary of Current Evidence
The included studies consistently demonstrate that both PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy and PD-1 inhibitor-based
combination therapy (typically with platinum-based chemotherapy) significantly improve overall survival and
progression-free survival compared with chemotherapy alone in patients with PD-L1-high (TPS ≥50%) metastatic
NSCLC [1–3]. A recurring finding across network meta-analyses and indirect comparisons is that combination therapy yields superior objective response rates and progression-free survival relative to monotherapy in PD-L1-high
patients [4–7], whereas overall survival differences between these two strategies are not statistically significant in
this subgroup [2, 4, 8, 15]. The KEYNOTE-598 trial established that adding ipilimumab to pembrolizumab confers no
efficacy benefit and increases toxicity [10, 11]. Current guidelines accordingly treat pembrolizumab monotherapy
and pembrolizumab–chemotherapy as complementary first-line options for PD-L1-high disease, with selection
guided by clinical context [9].

The evidence base is dominated by network meta-analyses and indirect comparisons (14 of 18 sources), with only
three primary RCTs (KEYNOTE-598 and its follow-up, and the epacadostat trial) providing direct head-to-head data,
none of which compared pembrolizumab monotherapy with pembrolizumab–chemotherapy [10–12]. The NMAs
consistently converge on the finding that combination therapy improves PFS (hazard ratios ranging from 0.55 to 0.81
across analyses) but does not significantly improve OS in PD-L1-high disease [2, 4, 6, 8]. However, almost all comparisons between monotherapy and chemo-immunotherapy are indirect, linked through a common chemotherapy
comparator, which introduces structural uncertainty that cannot be resolved without a direct randomized comparison.
11
Gaps in the Evidence Base
The most consequential gap is the absence of any completed, adequately powered, randomized controlled trial
directly comparing PD-1 inhibitor monotherapy with PD-1 inhibitor–chemotherapy in patients with PD-L1 TPS
≥50%. All efficacy comparisons between these strategies in the included literature derive from indirect methods
— network meta-analyses anchored to a chemotherapy common comparator [2, 4, 5, 7] — or from cross-trial
real-world comparisons subject to substantial confounding [9]. The only direct head-to-head RCTs examined
pembrolizumab–ipilimumab versus pembrolizumab alone (KEYNOTE-598) [10] and pembrolizumab–epacadostat
versus pembrolizumab alone [12], neither of which evaluated the clinically relevant pembrolizumab–chemotherapy
comparator. This means that the central clinical question — whether the PFS advantage of combination therapy
translates into meaningful long-term survival benefit or merely shifts disease control forward without altering
the OS trajectory — remains formally unanswered. The consequence is that clinician decision-making between
monotherapy and chemo-immunotherapy in PD-L1-high disease continues to rely on indirect evidence and clinical
judgment rather than definitive trial data.
Second, the evidence base lacks adequate characterization of patient subgroups most likely to benefit differentially
from monotherapy versus combination therapy. While several sources note that tumor burden, performance status,
age, and histology may modify the relative benefit of these strategies [5, 9, 10], no included study provides prospective, biomarker-stratified subgroup analyses of monotherapy versus chemo-immunotherapy within the PD-L1-high
population. Most NMAs do not report baseline characteristics such as liver metastases, brain metastases, or tumor
burden in sufficient detail to perform meaningful moderator analyses [2, 6, 8, 13]. Only two studies explicitly discuss
liver metastases as a clinical modifier [1, 14], and brain metastases subgroups are reported in only a few analyses
[10, 12, 14]. The absence of validated predictive biomarkers beyond PD-L1 TPS — such as tumor mutational burden,
gene expression profiles, or composite clinical-molecular scores — limits the ability to identify patients for whom
the added toxicity of chemotherapy is justified by a genuine survival benefit versus those for whom monotherapy
provides equivalent long-term outcomes with better tolerability [9, 13].
Third, safety data comparing monotherapy and combination therapy in the PD-L1-high subgroup specifically are
sparse. Most NMAs either do not report adverse events stratified by PD-L1 expression [6–8] or provide only aggregate safety data across PD-L1 strata [3, 13]. Detailed reporting of treatment-related deaths, discontinuation rates, and
immune-related adverse event profiles is available from only a few studies [10–12], and these evaluate combination
partners (ipilimumab, epacadostat) other than chemotherapy. The consequence is that the incremental toxicity burden of adding chemotherapy to pembrolizumab — particularly in older patients and those with ECOG performance
status 1 — cannot be precisely quantified for the PD-L1-high population from existing data, weakening the riskbenefit assessment that underpins treatment selection. Long-term quality-of-life and patient-reported outcome data
are similarly absent from all included sources, leaving the tolerability comparison between strategies incomplete.
Recommendations for Future Research
• Prospective randomized non-inferiority trial of pembrolizumab monotherapy versus pembrolizumab–
chemotherapy in PD-L1 TPS ≥50% NSCLC. The trial should enroll treatment-naive patients with metastatic
NSCLC, PD-L1 TPS ≥50%, and no targetable driver alterations. The primary endpoint should be overall
survival with a pre-specified non-inferiority margin (e.g., HR upper bound 1.20). Stratification by tumor
burden, histology, and performance status would enable prospective subgroup analyses. This design is
justified by the consistent NMA signal of equivalent OS between strategies [2, 4, 8] and addresses the most
critical evidence gap. The EA5163/S1709 INSIGNA trial has explored related sequencing questions [10], but a
definitive non-inferiority comparison remains needed.
12
• Biomarker-stratified observational cohort study integrating tumor mutational burden, gene expression signatures, and composite clinical scores. Given the repeated observation that PD-L1 TPS alone is insufficient
for treatment individualization [9, 13], a prospective registry enrolling patients receiving either monotherapy
or chemo-immunotherapy in routine practice should collect baseline tumor mutational burden, inflammatory
gene signatures, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and imaging-based tumor burden metrics. The primary objective would be to develop and validate a predictive model for differential OS benefit. This observational design
is feasible because both strategies are already in widespread clinical use, and the key barrier is systematic
biomarker collection rather than treatment randomization.
• Systematic safety comparison with patient-reported outcomes in PD-L1-high patients receiving monotherapy versus chemo-immunotherapy. A multi-center prospective cohort incorporating standardized adverse
event grading (CTCAE v5), immune-related adverse event phenotyping, treatment discontinuation tracking,
and validated patient-reported outcome instruments (e.g., EORTC QLQ-LC13) would address the current absence of quality-of-life data [9, 10]. Focus should include older adults (≥75 years) and patients with ECOG PS
1–2, populations frequently underrepresented in pivotal trials but commonly encountered in practice [9].
• Subgroup-focused analyses of existing trial data for patients with liver metastases, brain metastases,
and high tumor burden. Pooled individual patient data meta-analysis of KEYNOTE-024, KEYNOTE-042,
KEYNOTE-189, and KEYNOTE-407 could provide sufficiently powered subgroup comparisons that individual
NMAs cannot achieve with aggregate data [1, 14]. Given that liver metastases are associated with poorer
immunotherapy outcomes and brain metastases represent a growing clinical subpopulation, the absence of
stratified efficacy data for these groups directly limits evidence-based treatment selection.
• Cost-effectiveness analysis incorporating real-world effectiveness data and quality-of-life utilities. The
single included cost-effectiveness study relied on modeled clinical inputs from NMA rather than observed realworld outcomes [18]. A pragmatic economic evaluation using real-world data from health systems in diverse
geographic settings — including non-US populations underrepresented in the current evidence base — would
improve the generalizability of value assessments, particularly given the high incremental cost-effectiveness
ratios reported for pembrolizumab–chemotherapy versus monotherapy ICIs [18].
