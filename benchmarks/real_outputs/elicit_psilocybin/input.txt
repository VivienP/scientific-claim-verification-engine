What is the evidence for psilocybin in treatment-resistant depression?
Evidence from multiple trials including the largest randomized controlled trial demonstrates that psilocybin pro-
duces rapid, robust antidepressant effects in treatment-resistant depression, though sustained response is limited
and serious adverse events including suicidal ideation occurred in a minority of patients.
Abstract
Psilocybin demonstrates robust short-term antidepressant effects in treatment-resistant depression across 10 studies
comprising 5 open-label trials, 2 randomized controlled trials, and 3 secondary analyses. In the largest randomized
trial (n=233), a single 25 mg dose reduced depression scores significantly more than 1 mg control at 3 weeks (MADRS
difference -6.6, 95% CI -10.2 to -2.9, P<0.001) [1], with response rates of 37% versus 18% [1]. Open-label studies
reported even larger effect sizes ranging from Hedges' g=1.27 to 3.1 at 1-3 weeks [2, 3], though these likely reflect
placebo and expectancy effects alongside genuine treatment response. Depression reductions occurred within 1
week [2, 4, 5] and remained statistically significant at 6 months in follow-up studies (Cohen's d=1.4-1.5) [4, 5], but
sustained response rates at 12 weeks were only 20% in the randomized trial [1], indicating substantial relapse over
time. The quality of the acute psychedelic experience predicted clinical outcomes [2–4, 6], while comorbid PTSD
was associated with reduced treatment response [6].
Safety profiles were generally favorable in smaller studies with no serious adverse events [2–4, 7], but the largest trial
identified serious adverse events including suicidal ideation, intentional self-injury, and psychiatric hospitalization
[1], with worsening suicidal state in 14% of the 25 mg group [1]. Transient anxiety during dosing was universal [2, 4],
and common adverse events included headache (24%) and nausea (22%) [1]. The evidence base consists primarily of
patients with unipolar depression [1, 2, 4–6, 8] who had failed 2-5 previous treatments [1, 2, 4, 6], with systematic ex-
clusion of psychotic disorders [2, 4, 5], active substance use [2, 9], and high suicide risk in most studies [1]. Psilocybin
shows promise for treatment-resistant depression based on convergent short-term efficacy findings, but durability of
response, optimal dosing strategies, and safety in broader clinical populations require further investigation in larger
controlled trials with extended follow-up and active comparators.

The systematic review included 10 studies examining psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression, comprising 5
open-label trials, 2 randomized controlled trials, 2 secondary analyses, and 1 pilot trial. Sample sizes ranged from 7
to 233 participants. Two studies involved neuroimaging analyses of previously conducted trials.

The studies enrolled participants with moderate to severe depression at baseline. In studies reporting MADRS scores,
baseline means ranged from 31.7 to 33 [1, 7]. Studies using the QIDS-SR16 reported baseline scores of 18.9 [8], while
one study using the Beck Depression Inventory reported a mean baseline score of 34.81 [5]. Most participants had

Psilocybin Treatment Protocols
Psilocybin doses and administration varied across studies. The most common protocol involved two dosing sessions
of 10 mg and 25 mg administered 7 days apart [2–4]. The largest randomized trial compared single doses of 25 mg,
10 mg, and 1 mg (control) [1]. One study specifically examined severe TRD using a single 25 mg dose [6], while
another investigated one, two, or three 25 mg sessions over 6 months [9].

Effects on Depression Severity
Short-term Outcomes (1-3 Weeks)
Studies consistently demonstrated rapid reductions in depression severity. In the first open-label trial, mean QIDS
scores decreased by 11.8 points at 1 week post-treatment (95% CI -9.15 to -14.35, p=0.002, Hedges' g=3.1) [2]. The
largest randomized controlled trial found that 25 mg psilocybin reduced MADRS scores by 12.0 points at week 3,
compared to 5.4 points with 1 mg control (difference -6.6, 95% CI -10.2 to -2.9, P<0.001) [1]. The 10 mg dose showed
intermediate effects with a reduction of 7.9 points, though this did not differ significantly from the 1 mg control
(difference -2.5, 95% CI -6.2 to 1.2, P=0.18) [1].
In the study examining psilocybin adjunct to SSRIs, participants experienced a mean MADRS reduction of 14.9 points
at week 3 (95% CI -20.7 to -9.2) [7]. The severe TRD study reported MADRS least-squares mean changes of -15.8 at
week 3 (95% CI -25.4 to -6.3) [6]. A pilot trial found a mean QIDS change of -7.14 at 3 weeks (p=0.02, Hedges' g=-1.27,
95% CI -2.40 to -0.37) [3].
Neuroimaging studies corroborated clinical findings. One study using the Beck Depression Inventory reported a
mean reduction of 21.0 points at 1 week (Cohen's d=1.78, P<0.001) [5]. Another found QIDS-SR16 scores decreased
from baseline 18.9±3 to 8.8±6.2 at 1 week post-treatment (p<0.001) [8].
7

Medium-term Outcomes (5 Weeks to 3 Months)
Clinical improvements remained substantial at intermediate timepoints. At 5 weeks, the extended follow-up study
reported Cohen's d=2.3 for QIDS-SR16 reductions (p<0.001) [4], with 47% of patients meeting response criteria (≥50%
reduction) [8]. At 3 months, the original feasibility study showed mean QIDS reductions of 9.2 points (95% CI -5.69
to -12.71, p=0.003, Hedges' g=2) [2], with 58% maintaining response [2].
Long-term Outcomes (6 Months to 1 Year)
Longer-term follow-up revealed persistent but attenuating effects. At 6 months, effect sizes remained significant
with Cohen's d=1.4 (p<0.001) for QIDS-SR16 [4] and Cohen's d=1.07 (P<0.001) for BDI [5]. The severe TRD study
reported maintained improvements at 12 weeks with MADRS least-squares mean change of -17.2 (95% CI -25.2 to
-9.1) [6]. However, the largest RCT found sustained response rates at 12 weeks were only 20% for 25 mg, 5% for 10
mg, and 10% for 1 mg control [1], indicating substantial relapse over time.
Response and Remission Rates
Response and remission rates varied across studies and timepoints. At 3 weeks in the largest RCT, response rates
(≥50% MADRS reduction) were 37% for 25 mg, 19% for 10 mg, and 18% for 1 mg [1]. Remission rates (MADRS ≤10)
were 29% for 25 mg, 9% for 10 mg, and 8% for 1 mg [1]. The SSRI adjunct study found both response and remission
in 42.1% of participants at week 3 [7].
Earlier open-label studies reported higher rates: 67% achieved remission at 1 week in the initial feasibility trial [2],
while the 6-month follow-up study found 9 of 20 patients (45%) met response criteria at week 5, with 4 achieving
remission (20%) [4].
The pilot trial revealed marked individual variability: two participants showed sustained response, three relapsed,
and two exhibited no substantial improvement [3]. This heterogeneity in outcomes underscores the need for under-
standing response predictors.

Transient anxiety during drug onset was universal in studies reporting this outcome [2, 4]. Headache and nausea
were the most frequently reported treatment-emergent adverse events, affecting 22-33% of participants [1, 2]. These
events were typically mild and resolved within hours to days [2, 7]. Confusion or thought disorder occurred in 75%
of participants in one study but was transient [2]. No patients required tranquilizing medications during sessions in
the initial feasibility trial [2].
The largest randomized trial identified serious adverse events including suicidal ideation, intentional self-injury, and
hospitalization for severe depression [1]. Worsening of suicidal state occurred in 14% of the 25 mg group, 17% of the
10 mg group, and 9% of the 1 mg control group [1]. In contrast, another study found suicidal ideation was significantly
reduced at 1 and 2 weeks post-treatment [4], while the SSRI adjunct study reported no increase in suicidal ideation
during the study [7]. Study discontinuation rates due to adverse events ranged from 6% to 13% in the largest trial
[1], while most other studies reported no discontinuations [2, 7].
Factors Associated with Treatment Response
Several factors emerged as potential predictors or moderators of treatment response, though findings varied across
studies.
9

Psychedelic Experience Characteristics: The quality and intensity of the acute psychedelic experience correlated
with clinical outcomes in multiple studies. One study found that reductions in depressive symptoms at 5 weeks were
predicted by the quality of the acute psychedelic experience [2], with significant relationships between the ”USB”
factor (experience of unity, spiritual experience, blissful state) and symptom changes [4]. The Oceanic Boundlessness
dimension of the psychedelic experience correlated with post-dosing antidepressant responses in severe TRD [6]. A
pilot trial identified mindset prior to dosing, spiritual experiences, and perceptual shifts during dosing as predictors
of treatment trajectory, while treatment expectations were not a reliable predictor [3]. The intensity of altered or
mystical states of consciousness was noted as correlating with therapeutic outcomes [7].
Neural Mechanisms: Neuroimaging studies identified brain connectivity changes associated with treatment re-
sponse. Increased ventromedial prefrontal cortex-bilateral inferior lateral parietal cortex resting-state functional
connectivity predicted response at 5 weeks, as did decreased parahippocampal-prefrontal cortex connectivity [8].
Decreased brain network modularity was associated with improved clinical outcomes and predicted sustained im-
provements in depression severity [5], with early-phase modularity changes being predictive of long-term response
[5]. Changes in network flexibility, particularly in the executive and salience networks, were identified as key com-
ponents of the therapeutic mechanism [5].
Comorbidity Effects: Patients with comorbid PTSD (n=5) showed significantly less antidepressant effect of psilocy-
bin [6], suggesting this comorbidity may moderate treatment response. Studies generally excluded individuals with
psychotic disorders [2, 4, 5] and some excluded substance use disorders [2, 9], limiting knowledge about psilocybin's
effects in these populations.
Dose Effects: Pharmacokinetic research showed dose-dependent increases in receptor occupancy and subjective
effects [1], with the 25 mg dose demonstrating superior efficacy to 10 mg and 1 mg in the largest trial [1]. Repeated
doses were associated with further reductions in MADRS scores compared to baseline [9], though specific dose-
response relationships require further investigation.
Individual Variability: One pilot study documented diverse response patterns, with two participants showing sus-
tained response, three experiencing relapse, and two exhibiting no substantial improvement [3]. This heterogeneity
suggests important individual differences in treatment response that warrant further study.
Synthesis
The evidence base for psilocybin in treatment-resistant depression reveals consistently robust short-term effects
across diverse study designs and populations, but substantial heterogeneity in sustained response rates and concern-
ing safety signals in the largest randomized trial merit careful interpretation.
Short-term Efficacy: Convergent Evidence Across Study Designs
Effect sizes for depression reduction at 1-3 weeks were remarkably consistent across methodologically diverse studies.
Open-label trials reported effect sizes ranging from Hedges' g=1.27 to 3.1 [2, 3], while the phase 2 RCT demonstrated
a statistically significant 6.6-point greater MADRS reduction with 25 mg versus 1 mg control (P<0.001) [1]. Even
accounting for potential placebo effects in open-label designs and the active control effect of 1 mg psilocybin, the
convergence of findings across blinded and unblinded studies supports genuine short-term antidepressant efficacy.
The response occurred within 1 week in most studies [2, 4, 5], distinguishing psilocybin from conventional antide-
pressants which typically require weeks to months for therapeutic effects.
Durability: Attenuation Over Time
The apparent contradiction between open-label studies showing sustained effects at 6 months (Cohen's d=1.4-1.5) [4,
10

5] and the RCT showing only 20% sustained response at 12 weeks [1] may reflect several factors. First, open-label
designs likely capture additional placebo effects and expectancy biases that inflate durability estimates. Second, the
RCT's stringent definition of sustained response (meeting criteria at week 3 and all subsequent visits) may under-
estimate partial or fluctuating responses that still represent clinical improvement. Third, sample enrichment may
have occurred in open-label studies through selective reporting or participant retention, while the RCT's systematic
follow-up captured all patterns including non-responders and relapsers.
The trajectory data suggest initial benefit followed by gradual relapse for many patients. Effect sizes decreased from
2.3 at week 5 to 1.5 at 3 months and 1.4 at 6 months in one study [4], indicating statistical significance was maintained
but clinical magnitude diminished. This pattern suggests psilocybin may provide a ”window of opportunity” for
neuroplastic change rather than indefinite symptom suppression, aligning with proposed ”reset” mechanisms [8]
and neural network integration changes [5]. Repeated dosing strategies showed promise for enhancing durability
[9], though optimal timing and frequency remain undefined.
Dose-Response and Protocol Optimization
The 25 mg dose demonstrated clear superiority to 1 mg control [1], while 10 mg showed intermediate effects that
did not reach statistical significance versus control [1]. This suggests a dose threshold for reliable therapeutic effects
between 10-25 mg in single-dose paradigms. However, the two-dose protocol (10 mg followed by 25 mg seven
days later) used in multiple successful studies [2–4] may provide advantages over single dosing through gradual
dose escalation and repeated psychedelic experiences. The feasibility of repeated dosing over 6 months [9, 9] and
preliminary evidence for cumulative benefits [9] warrant investigation of maintenance dosing strategies.
The demonstration that psilocybin can be administered safely adjunct to ongoing SSRI treatment [7, 7] has important
clinical implications, as antidepressant withdrawal may be problematic or contraindicated for many TRD patients.
While SSRIs theoretically attenuate 5-HT2A receptor activation, therapeutic effects were observed in this paradigm
[7], suggesting either incomplete receptor blockade or therapeutic mechanisms beyond acute 5-HT2A agonism.
Safety Considerations and Clinical Vigilance
The divergent safety findings between the largest RCT showing serious adverse events including suicidal ideation
and self-injury [1] and smaller studies showing no serious events [2–4, 7] likely reflect several factors. First, the
larger sample (n=233) provided greater statistical power to detect rare serious events occurring at 1-2% base rates.
Second, the 12-week follow-up period captured delayed-onset events beyond the acute treatment phase when most
smaller studies ended follow-up. Third, the RCT's systematic safety monitoring may have identified events that went
unreported or were attributed to underlying depression in less rigorous designs.
The finding that worsening suicidal state occurred across all dose groups including 1 mg control (9-17%) [1] suggests
these events may represent the natural course of severe TRD rather than psilocybin-specific toxicity. However, the
numerical trend toward higher rates in active treatment groups (14-17% vs 9%) and the occurrence of intentional
self-injury and hospitalization indicate careful patient selection and monitoring protocols are essential. Studies
excluding patients at significant suicide risk [1] demonstrated favorable safety profiles [2, 4, 7], suggesting this
exclusion criterion should be maintained in clinical applications.
The universal occurrence of transient anxiety during drug onset [2, 4] and high rates of thought disorder or confusion
(75%) [2] underscore the necessity of trained therapeutic support during dosing sessions. The lack of serious adverse
events during supervised dosing sessions across all studies [1–4, 7] validates the safety of the therapeutic model
when properly implemented.
Predictors and Mechanisms: Toward Personalized Treatment
11

The quality and content of the acute psychedelic experience consistently predicted treatment outcomes across studies
[2–4, 6, 7], suggesting therapeutic effects depend partially on subjective phenomenology rather than pharmacology
alone. This aligns with the importance of ”set and setting” in psychedelic therapy and supports the integration of
preparatory and integration psychotherapy [1–4, 6, 7] as essential treatment components rather than mere safety
measures.
The identification of comorbid PTSD as a negative predictor [6] suggests trauma-related symptoms may interfere
with psilocybin's antidepressant mechanisms or that depression secondary to PTSD represents a distinct syndrome
requiring modified treatment approaches. This finding requires replication but has immediate clinical relevance for
patient selection.
Neural mechanisms involving decreased network modularity [5], increased default mode network connectivity [8],
and changes in executive and salience network flexibility [5] provide convergent evidence for psilocybin's effects
on large-scale brain network organization. These findings support theoretical models proposing therapeutic benefit
through enhanced neural flexibility and integration, potentially explaining both rapid onset (acute network pertur-
bation) and gradual relapse (reversion to maladaptive patterns without sustained neuroplastic consolidation).
Population Considerations
The evidence base encompasses general TRD (≥2 failed treatments) [1, 2, 4] and severe TRD (≥5 failed treatments)
[6], with both populations demonstrating treatment response. However, baseline characteristics varied substantially,
with mean failed medications ranging from 2 to 4.6 [1, 2, 4, 5]. Most studies enrolled primarily unipolar depression
[1, 2, 4–6, 8], with only one study explicitly including bipolar II disorder [9]. The generalizability of findings to
bipolar depression, psychotic depression, and depression with active substance use disorders remains unknown due
to systematic exclusion of these populations [1, 2, 9].
Most participants were White (78-92%) [1, 7] and many studies were conducted in single countries or limited geo-
graphic regions, raising questions about cross-cultural generalizability of psychedelic therapy paradigms that may
depend on cultural context and expectancy effects.
